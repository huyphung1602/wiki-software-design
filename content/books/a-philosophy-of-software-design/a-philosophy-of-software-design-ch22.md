# Chapter 22 - Conclusion

Part of [[a-philosophy-of-software-design]]

The book's single subject is [[complexity]], the most important challenge in software design. The conclusion revisits the three major threads: root causes ([[dependencies-and-obscurity]]), [[red-flags|red flags]] for identifying unnecessary complexity, and the design ideas that combat it ([[deep-modules|deep classes]], [[exception-handling|defining errors out of existence]], separating interface from implementation documentation).

## The Cost and Reward of Good Design

All the book's suggestions create extra work early in a project, and learning design techniques initially slows you down. But the investment pays off quickly: carefully defined modules get reused, clear documentation saves time on return visits, and honed design skills produce good designs faster. Good design does not really take much longer than quick-and-dirty design once you know how.

## Design as a Creative Act

If good design is an important goal, the ideas in this book make programming more fun. Design is a puzzle: how can a problem be solved with the simplest possible structure? It is satisfying to explore different approaches and discover a solution that is both simple and powerful. A clean, simple, and [[obvious-code|obvious]] design is a beautiful thing.

The ultimate reward for being a good designer is spending more time in the design phase -- which is where the intellectual satisfaction lives. Poor designers spend their time chasing bugs in complicated, brittle code. Improving design skills means producing higher quality software more quickly, and enjoying the process more.
